# Route 3: Faculty Outreach & Cold Emailing Guide
### Direct Faculty Contact, In-Person Meetings, & Outreach Strategy

[← Back to Main Menu](./index.md)

---

While cold emailing is a standard route into research, **meeting a professor face-to-face is often significantly more effective**. Faculty receive dozens of emails daily, and cold inquiries can easily get buried in an inbox. Meeting in person lets you demonstrate genuine enthusiasm, stand out from the crowd, and build immediate personal rapport.

---

## Step 1: Literature Grounding (Pre-Meeting Preparation)

Whether you meet in person or email first, spend at least 15–20 minutes reviewing the faculty member's recent work:
* Visit their **lab website**, UC Berkeley profile, or Google Scholar profile.
* Read the abstract and intro/conclusion of **1 or 2 recent papers** (published within the last 2–3 years). You can read more, but this is a good baseline.
* Identify **one specific sub-project, methodology, or dataset** that genuinely excites you or connects to your coursework.

---

## Step 2: In-Person First Strategy (Office Hours & Office Visits)

Whenever possible, prioritize meeting a professor in person—either during their scheduled office hours or by dropping by their office/lab space.

### 1. Attending Public Office Hours (Highest Success Rate)
* **How to Find Them:** Check the department website, course syllabi, or the main office bulletin board for public teaching/departmental office hours.
* **The Approach:** Go prepared with 1–2 specific questions about their published research or recent public talks—**do not lead by immediately asking for a lab position**. 
* **The Transition:** After discussing the paper or topic, transition naturally: 
  > *"I’ve really enjoyed learning about your work on [Topic]. I’m very interested in getting hands-on experience in this field—are you currently taking on any undergraduate researchers in your lab?"*

### 2. Dropping By Their Office or Lab
* **Best Timing:** Aim for mid-morning or early afternoon on non-lecture days, or right after a department seminar.
* **The 2-Minute Elevator Pitch:** Keep your intro brief and respectful of their time:
  > *"Hi Professor [Last Name], my name is [Your Name]. I'm an undergrad in [Major] really interested in your work on [Topic]. I sent a brief email recently, but I wanted to stop by for just two minutes to introduce myself in person and see if you might have space for an undergraduate researcher this term."*
* **If They Are Busy:** If they are in the middle of work or meeting with someone, apologize politely, hand them a printed copy of your 1-page resume, and ask if there is a better time to come back or if they prefer to coordinate over email.

---

## Step 3: Cold Email Template (If In-Person Isn't Immediately Possible)

If you cannot find their office hours or office location, keep your outreach email concise (under 200–250 words) so it can be read quickly on a phone screen. Also, while this template can be used, it is beneficial to give your emails a little personality! PIs typically get a ton of emails, so making yours stand out is important.

> **Subject:** Prospective Undergraduate Researcher – [Your Major] Student Interested in [Specific Topic]
>
> Dear Professor [Last Name],
>
> My name is [Your Name], and I am a [Year, e.g., second-year] majoring in [Your Major] at UC Berkeley. I recently read your publication on [Briefly state topic/paper title] and was particularly interested in [Mention 1 specific detail, method, or finding from their work].
>
> I am writing to ask if you have any open positions for an undergraduate researcher in your lab for [Term/Semester, e.g., Fall 2026]. My goal is to contribute to research in [Field/Topic] while building practical experience. 
>
> Relevant background I bring includes:
> * **Coursework:** [List 1–2 relevant courses, e.g., Physics 7B, CS 61A, Math 54]
> * **Technical Skills:** [List tools, e.g., Python, LaTeX, wet lab pipetting, data analysis]
> * **Availability:** I can commit [e.g., 8–10] hours per week.
>
> I have attached my resume for your review. If you have time, I would love to drop by your office hours or set up a brief 10-minute meeting to introduce myself and discuss how I might support your lab's ongoing projects.
>
> Thank you for your time and consideration.
>
> Best regards,  
>
> **[Your Name]**  
> [Your Email] | [Your Major / Year] | [Link to LinkedIn or GitHub if applicable]

---

## Step 4: Follow-Up & Tracking Protocol

* **The 7-to-10 Day Rule:** If an email goes unanswered, try to stop by their office or office hours in person first. If that isn't feasible, send a short, polite follow-up replying directly to your original thread:
  > *"Dear Professor [Last Name], I am following up on my previous email regarding prospective undergraduate research opportunities in your lab. I know you have a busy schedule, so I just wanted to resend this in case it got buried. Thank you again for your time!"*
* **Track Your Outreach:** Keep a simple spreadsheet recording:
  * Faculty Name & Department
  * Office Location & Office Hour Schedule
  * Date Emailed / Date Visited in Person
  * Paper/Project Referenced
  * Response Status
